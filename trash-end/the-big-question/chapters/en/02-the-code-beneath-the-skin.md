Then I came across a news item about induced regeneration in some animals. It opened a question I could not close: which animals can already regenerate their brains? I went looking and found planarians, small flatworms capable of rebuilding their head and nervous system from fragments of their body.

In 2013, Tal Shomrat and Michael Levin trained planarians to become familiar with a feeding environment with a rough surface. After decapitation and head regeneration, previously trained animals recovered the behavior faster than controls in a *savings* test, meaning a test of relearning<sup>[10]</sup>. It was not a maze, and the result does not show that a complete memory remains intact while the brain is replaced. It shows a behavioral effect consistent with the persistence, or reconstruction, of something acquired before decapitation.

The documented finding ends there. The study does not establish where the information was stored or which mechanism made it available to the regenerated brain. Nervous tissue remaining in the body, biochemical or epigenetic changes, and other distributed states are possibilities to distinguish, not an explanation already settled.

Bioelectricity requires the same caution. Patterns of potential between cells participate in morphogenesis, and a 2025 modeling study shows how an electric field can contribute to the organization of bioelectric prepatterns<sup>[2]</sup>. That work concerns the shape a tissue builds, not the memory of a learned experience. Saying a tissue “remembers” a morphology and saying an animal remembers an environment uses the same word for two different phenomena.

A 2026 preprint also shows that gene silencing through interfering RNA can persist through regeneration<sup>[1]</sup>. This is another form of biological continuity, but it is not behavioral memory and does not, by itself, explain the 2013 result.

The most cautious conclusion is also the most interesting: in planarians, a regenerated brain can recover a behavior learned before decapitation more quickly, while the physical support of that continuity remains an open question. It does not prove that the brain is merely an interface. It does force us to ask how much a memory depends on the present brain and how much on the history of the whole organism.

To quantify this “depth of history” without getting lost in overly vague definitions of complexity, we can draw on Walker and Cronin's **Assembly Theory**. The theory proposes a mathematical index of the minimum number of recursive steps needed to construct a structure from its basic elements. A high Assembly Index does not easily arise by chance: it suggests a physical generator, such as biological evolution or technological iteration, capable of storing history and executing instructions.

It is a real framework, though debated, developed mainly in research on the origins of life. Here I am using it as a lens, not as proof.

In this framework, cells are not static bricks. As Michael Levin and Karl Friston show, cell collectives can be read through **scale-free active inference**, under the Free Energy Principle. They do not simply react to bioelectric voltage gradients (Vmem)<sup>[2]</sup>; they seem to negotiate toward a “target morphology,” maintaining a distributed predictive model of the shape to be reached.

Michael Levin at Tufts University has been building this case for years. His work on bioelectricity, xenobots, and morphogenetic fields shows that intelligence operates at scales far below and far above the neuron. Cells compute. Tissues remember. Bodies navigate toward form without a central plan. Levin calls it “mind everywhere.” Not poetically, but operationally: as distributed information processing.

Sara Imari Walker<sup>[3]</sup> goes further: life *is* information that has learned to resist entropy. Not information *about* life. Information that *becomes* life. The distinction matters.

Here the relatively solid ground ends and the leap begins: if life is fundamentally informational, perhaps the boundary between biological and artificial intelligence is not a wall. It might be a gradient. This proves nothing about current AI. But it changes the shape of the question.
