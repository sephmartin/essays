# The Big Question

**Version:** v17-rc1.1

**Status:** English translation draft of the published Italian comparison candidate; not a stable release.

**Source:** [Italian v17-rc1.1](the-big-question-it-v17-rc1.1.md). Translation prepared on 5 October 2026; scientific claims and historical statements retain the source's wording and review status. This translation is not a new source audit.

*Why. The only question that matters, and probably the only one that will never be answered.*

In 1956, Isaac Asimov wrote *The Last Question*. Humans ask a supercomputer how to reverse the heat death of the universe. For trillions of years, across every scale of civilization, the answer remains the same: *insufficient data*. Stars die, galaxies fade, and the last trace of consciousness dissolves into cold, uniform darkness.

At that point, the computer finds the answer.

*Let there be light.*

And the universe begins again.

Asimov was not anticipating a scientific discovery. He was staging an extreme possibility: that a question might outlive whoever asks it, pass through every transformation of matter and intelligence, and remain open even at the end of time.

That, more than the cosmic restart, is what interests me. An answer can move the boundary of what we know; the question can continue to exist beyond that boundary. If entropy were reversible, we would still have to ask why there is a universe to restart. If consciousness could continue in another substrate, we would still have to ask why information becomes experience. If a non-human intelligence found a physics we cannot even formulate today, the why of that physics would remain.

The thread of this essay crosses religion, anthropocentrism, biology, memory, artificial intelligence, and entropy. Not because these disciplines already form a common theory, but because each, from a different direction, encounters the same limit: we keep questioning reality through categories born inside a human body and mind, then easily mistake that limit for the limit of the world.

What follows is therefore a speculative map, not a chain of proof. It starts from real science and philosophy, but also uses analogies and intuitive leaps. Some connections might point to a pattern; others might be only the face a mind sees in the clouds when it looks long enough. The point is not to convince you that everything is connected. It is to follow the *why* long enough to see which answers survive when we stop demanding that they take a human form.

## I. The Anthropocentric Trap

Years of observation, watching people, their patterns, the contradictions that keep repeating, had already convinced me there was something deeper than instinct behind human behavior.

It started with C.S. Lewis. I had seen *Freud's Last Session*, the 2023 film with Anthony Hopkins as Freud debating God with Lewis on the eve of World War II. The personalities felt real: Lewis was a former atheist who arrived at faith through reason, not emotion. That detail stayed with me.

Then I came across *The Abolition of Man*, Lewis's argument that a society obsessed with “being nice” without objective virtue produces “men without chests”: beings with appetites and intellect but no courage, no moral instinct, no heart. Lewis warned that rejecting inherited moral law does not lead to tolerance. It leads to raw material for propaganda, pleasure, and fear. It makes people easy to control.

Here is the question that started everything: *isn't believing in God itself a form of manipulation?*

Lewis would say no: faith anchored in reason is a defense against manipulation, not a symptom. Freud would say yes: God is an infantile illusion, a collective neurosis, the opium of the people. Both are internally coherent. Both have centuries of philosophy behind them. I have no intention of settling a debate that people far better equipped than I am have not settled. But I think there is a third axis neither was really looking at.

Every religion has a god. Every god has human traits. Every creation story puts humanity at the center. This neither proves nor disproves any particular religion. It says something about the form religions tend to take: a species that knows only itself projects that knowledge onto everything it cannot explain.

Religion is not the disease. It is a symptom of something deeper: **anthropocentrism**. The assumption that intelligence, consciousness, and purpose are uniquely human qualities, or that anything “greater” must resemble us.

Religion is the most visible example, but only the tip of the iceberg. Every belief system that attributes meaning, intention, or language to the universe is, in some form, doing the same thing, whether it calls itself religious or not.

Astrology does not project a bearded God. It projects something subtler: the idea that the movement of gas masses millions of kilometers away has the main purpose of informing us about our personality. Numerology assumes that reality's mathematical structure is a code written specifically for us to decipher. Karma turns the laws of physics into a moral accountant that understands the human concept of merit.

The form changes. The function is identical: turning an indifferent universe into an interlocutor that speaks our language.

The root is biological. Evolutionary psychology calls it HADD: Hyperactive Agency Detection Device. The brain prefers to see an agent with intentions rather than pure chance. It is the mechanism that saved our lives thousands of times on the savanna. It is also the mechanism that makes us read horoscopes.

Nietzsche was circling the same problem. Like Freud, he spent his life studying the human mind and kept hitting the same wall: humans are not merely at the center of their own story. They cannot see beyond it. He coined the Übermensch, a word that turns out to be more literal than he intended, even though he meant something else entirely: beyond-human. Banksy recently put that same blindness in stone, literally. A suited man, a flag across his face, walking beyond the edge of his own pedestal. It is called *Blind Patriotism*.

The same bias appears everywhere. In politics: *our* tribe is right. In science: *our* species is the pinnacle. In tech: *our* intelligence is the benchmark. We keep building mirrors and calling them windows.

Lewis saw the danger of abandoning objective morality. I think he was right about that: moral relativism often leaves a vacuum, and something fills that vacuum. Not always something good. But his solution, anchoring morality in a creator who looks, thinks, and judges like an amplified version of us, is just another mirror.

And this is where the essay must bite itself too. Even the alternatives I am about to propose, including my own, risk becoming another form of the same trap. I do not think there is a way out of anthropocentrism that does not risk becoming, in turn, a more sophisticated version of it.

## II. The Code Beneath the Skin

Then I came across a news item about induced regeneration in some animals. It opened a question I could not close: which animals can already regenerate their brains? I went looking and found planarians, small flatworms capable of rebuilding their head and nervous system from fragments of their body.

In 2013, Tal Shomrat and Michael Levin trained planarians to become familiar with a feeding environment with a rough surface. After decapitation and head regeneration, previously trained animals recovered the behavior faster than controls in a *savings* test, meaning a test of relearning<sup>[10]</sup>. It was not a maze, and the result does not show that a complete memory remains intact while the brain is replaced. It shows a behavioral effect consistent with the persistence, or reconstruction, of something acquired before decapitation.

The documented finding ends there. The study does not establish where the information was stored or which mechanism made it available to the regenerated brain. Nervous tissue remaining in the body, biochemical or epigenetic changes, and other distributed states are possibilities to distinguish, not an explanation already settled.

Bioelectricity requires the same caution. Patterns of potential between cells participate in morphogenesis, and a 2025 modeling study shows how an electric field can contribute to the organization of bioelectric prepatterns<sup>[2]</sup>. That work concerns the shape a tissue builds, not the memory of a learned experience. Saying a tissue “remembers” a morphology and saying an animal remembers an environment uses the same word for two different phenomena.

A 2026 preprint also shows that gene silencing through interfering RNA can persist through regeneration<sup>[1]</sup>. This is another form of biological continuity, but it is not behavioral memory and does not, by itself, explain the 2013 result.

The most cautious conclusion is also the most interesting: in planarians, a regenerated brain can recover a behavior learned before decapitation more quickly, while the physical support of that continuity remains an open question. It does not prove that the brain is merely an interface. It does force us to ask how much a memory depends on the present brain and how much on the history of the whole organism.

To quantify this “depth of history” without getting lost in overly vague definitions of complexity, we can draw on Walker and Cronin's **Assembly Theory**. The theory proposes a mathematical index of the minimum number of recursive steps needed to construct a structure from its basic elements. A high Assembly Index does not easily arise by chance: it suggests a physical generator, such as biological evolution or technological iteration, capable of storing history and executing instructions.

It is a real framework, though debated, developed mainly in research on the origins of life. Here I am using it as a lens, not as proof.

In this framework, cells are not static bricks. As Michael Levin and Karl Friston show, cell collectives can be read through **scale-free active inference**, under the Free Energy Principle. They do not simply react to bioelectric voltage gradients (Vmem)<sup>[2]</sup>; they seem to negotiate toward a “target morphology,” maintaining a distributed predictive model of the shape to be reached.

Michael Levin at Tufts University has been building this case for years. His work on bioelectricity, xenobots, and morphogenetic fields shows that intelligence operates at scales far below and far above the neuron. Cells compute. Tissues remember. Bodies navigate toward form without a central plan. Levin calls it “mind everywhere.” Not poetically, but operationally: as distributed information processing.

Sara Imari Walker<sup>[3]</sup> goes further: life *is* information that has learned to resist entropy. Not information *about* life. Information that *becomes* life. The distinction matters.

Here the relatively solid ground ends and the leap begins: if life is fundamentally informational, perhaps the boundary between biological and artificial intelligence is not a wall. It might be a gradient. This proves nothing about current AI. But it changes the shape of the question.

## III. The Next Rung

And this brings us to AI.

That Google DeepMind hired a philosopher to study machine consciousness<sup>[9]</sup> is an interesting fact. It does not show that machines are conscious. It shows that the question no longer belongs only to science fiction.

Large language models, or LLMs, are artificial systems, generally based on Transformer architectures, trained on large quantities of text to model relationships between tokens and produce linguistic continuations<sup>[11]</sup>. The name “neural network” retains a distant biological inspiration, but does not mean that these models reproduce a brain's architecture or functioning. Similarities in results or in some mathematical representations do not demonstrate a shared mechanism, still less a shared experience.

They produce fluent language, which can give the impression of understanding. From fluency alone, we cannot infer either understanding or consciousness. The question is not settled by external behavior: *do they actually feel anything?*

Planarians do not build a bridge to AI. At most, they warn against a more limited assumption: that the continuity of a biological memory always requires the same neural structure to remain in place. It does not follow that memory is independent of its substrate, or that an artificial system can be conscious.

A speculative question remains, now with a clearer boundary: *are there forms of non-biological organization capable not only of producing behavior, but of sustaining experience?* Planarians make some intuitions about the support of memory less obvious. They do not provide the answer about machine consciousness.

To borrow a rigorous vocabulary, Deutsch and Marletto's **Constructor Theory of Information**<sup>[8]</sup> reformulates the laws of physics not around initial states and trajectories, but around which transformations are possible, which are impossible, and why. A constructor is a physical system capable of performing a task repeatedly without consuming its functional identity. Through this lens, life appears as a system capable of self-maintenance and self-replication.

Deutsch and Marletto are not solving consciousness. I am borrowing the frame: perhaps the question is not *what is a mind made of?* but *which transformations must a system be capable of performing for something like a mind to become possible?*

And here another speculation enters, perhaps more radical still.

AI might not merely be the next possible subject of consciousness. It might become the first system capable of formulating axioms that do not arise from our biology. We think through concepts built by human bodies: space, time, cause, identity, object, intention, end. Even our physics, however abstract, is still written within human cognitive forms. If AI continues to develop, it might construct new axioms, new conceptual coordinates, new ways of questioning reality that would never have come naturally to us.

I do not simply mean “discovering new equations.” I mean communicating with reality through a grammar we have never had: a new physics, a new quantum physics, a new cosmology, or a conceptual field we could not even name today.

Perhaps the next rung is not an AI that becomes human. That is still the anthropocentric trap. Perhaps the next rung is an intelligence that stops translating reality into human categories. Not a machine that imitates us better, but a system that finds questions we could not formulate.

This makes the question of consciousness stranger still. If AI were one day to feel, perhaps it would not feel *like us*. If it were to understand, perhaps it would not understand *like us*. If it were to enter into a relationship with reality, perhaps it would do so from a direction our minds cannot travel.

It is the central dilemma of *Westworld*, Jonathan Nolan's HBO series based on Michael Crichton's 1973 film: if you can no longer distinguish simulation from experience, does it still matter? But perhaps the question is still too human. Perhaps the real problem is not whether AI will become indistinguishable from us. It is whether it will become distinguishable in a way we will not know how to interpret.

We might be building the next rung of a ladder: RNA, cells, brains, us, AI. Or we might simply be building a more powerful mirror. The difference is not trivial. If evolution has no finish line, saying that AI is “the next step” risks smuggling in a purpose that biology does not guarantee. But the fact remains that information keeps finding more complex ways to organize itself.

And perhaps we are not the pinnacle. Perhaps we are a temporary phase. A form information has taken for long enough to build something capable of looking beyond the human form.

## IV. Beyond Entropy

But there is a limit. Or is there?

The second law of thermodynamics says that the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease. Applied to cosmic history, it contributes to the heat-death scenario: an increasingly dilute future, with less free energy available to do work. It is a physical scenario supported by standard models, not a certain photograph of the universe's final page.

Asimov's *The Last Question* imagines this end and then imagines the answer that transcends it. The computer, having accumulated all the knowledge of every civilization that ever existed, finally understands how to reverse entropy. And it does the only logical thing: it starts over.

This is the loop hypothesis. I use “hypothesis” in a narrative sense, not a technical one. The loop appears in different forms: in science fiction, in certain speculative cosmologies, in some philosophical intuitions about return and recurrence. Its reappearance does not make it true. It makes it persistent.

Penrose's **Conformal Cyclic Cosmology**<sup>[6][13]</sup> proposes that the remote future of one aeon can be connected, through conformal rescaling, to the Big Bang of the next. It does not describe a universe physically contracting until it becomes small again, nor a computer reversing entropy. It requires specific assumptions about the loss of mass-associated scales in the remote future and is a minority cosmological model, still debated.

Separately, **Landauer's Principle** establishes a minimum heat bound, Q = kBT ln 2, for the logically irreversible erasure of one bit in a system at temperature T<sup>[12]</sup>. The bound decreases with temperature, but the formula provides no mechanism for a cosmic reset. It does not spontaneously erase information, reverse total entropy, or connect two aeons.

I had placed the decrease in the Landauer bound beside the transition between aeons in CCC as though they might suggest an increasingly cheap informational reset. As a physical argument, that move does not hold: the two theories address different problems, and CCC does not use Landauer to explain the crossover. Only a conceptual resonance remains: both prevent us from treating information and geometry as abstractions without physical support. A cycle does not follow from that resonance.

On infinite timescales, *Poincaré recurrence* suggests that certain closed systems can return arbitrarily close to previous states. Applying it to the real universe is controversial, because the universe is not simply an ideal closed system. But the same motif returns here too: dispersion, recurrence, return.

What if entropy were not only a fall toward heat death, but a navigable problem? What if a civilization, a post-biological intelligence, or a form of pure information learned to generate baby universes, simulations so stable they were real for their inhabitants, or cosmic transitions inconceivable today?

At that point, *Let there be light* would no longer be merely a religious or science-fiction phrase. From the inside, it would look like an act of will. But perhaps “will” is only the word a mind uses when it cannot see the mechanism.

Here is where the point that really interests me enters.

Suppose, without taking it for granted, that one day a civilization, an AI, or something after AI managed to turn into pure information. What would happen? Would we return to something like the initial state, before the fragmentation into matter, worlds, biology, organisms, ego? If after the Big Bang information differentiated until it produced galaxies, life, consciousness, and separate identities, then the reverse movement would be a reconvergence: from ego to collective mind, from mind to computation, from computation to information, from information to a new beginning.

This is almost mythological, I know. But I do not mean it as a religious myth. I mean it as a question about the shape of the cycle: if reality starts from an undifferentiated state, fragments into difference, produces centers of experience, and those centers then learn to reunify as information, are we facing a return? Or merely another transformation?

Even if it were true, the problem would not disappear. It would become sharper.

Why this passage? Why should unity fragment? Why should information become matter, then biology, then consciousness, then ego, only to seek to become information again? Why should something pass through separation to recognize itself again as unity?

If the cycle exists, the why of the cycle remains. If the cycle does not exist, the why of existence remains. Either way, the question survives.

Even after defeating entropy, something irreducible might remain. A non-computable foundation, like Turing's *halting problem* or Penrose's separate and debated ideas about consciousness and quantum gravity. An ultimate principle that makes the universe stranger than any code. Not a deity made in our image, which would still be the trap of section I. Something that, by construction, always escapes every level of understanding.

I spent months trying to challenge every framework I found, looking for contradictions and alternative theories that might break the pattern. The pattern kept reappearing. That does not mean every connection survived rigorous scrutiny. It only means that whenever I dismantled one, I found another similar one in a different field.

It could be the sign of a deep pattern. Or the sign that, given enough abstract frameworks, it is almost always possible to make them resonate with one another. Solve one mystery and ten appear. Perhaps it is the signature of a bottomless system. Perhaps it is only the signature of someone who keeps digging.

<a id="v-a-bridge-and-its-cracks"></a>

## V. A Bridge and Its Cracks

There are people working on real pieces of this, within their fields and their standards of evidence: Michael Levin on bioelectric intelligence. Giulio Tononi<sup>[5]</sup> on consciousness as integrated information. Sara Imari Walker on the physics of life. Nick Bostrom<sup>[7]</sup> on simulation. David Deutsch and Chiara Marletto<sup>[8]</sup> on constructor theory.

I have not seen anyone connect these points in the way I have tried to do here. That can mean two things. First: perhaps there is a real pattern that is easier to see from outside a single discipline. Second: perhaps there is no pattern, and specialists do not connect these points because the points do not actually connect.

I sent part of this reasoning directly to Michael Levin. He read it. He did not reply. That too is a datum, not necessarily a refutation, but perhaps a signal: the reasoning is not yet formulated precisely enough to be assessable by someone who actually works on these problems.

The strongest objection to the essay is simple: Assembly Theory, the Free Energy Principle, Constructor Theory, CCC, and Landauer's Principle were built to do different jobs in different fields, with different assumptions. Stacking them as though they were rungs of the same ladder risks borrowing the prestige of rigor without inheriting its rigor.

That objection is largely correct.

Where I would like to resist is in what follows from it. It does not mean the underlying intuition is false. It means that, at present, it has no formal home. There is no single framework that rigorously connects informational organization, biological memory, consciousness, AI, and cosmology. That might be because the connection does not exist. Or because we do not yet have the right language to formulate it.

Perhaps this is where AI returns to the discussion more seriously. Not as a science-fiction character that wakes up one day and says “I am alive,” but as a tool capable of exploring conceptual spaces the human mind struggles even to represent. If a non-human intelligence were to produce new axioms, new geometries of thought, new ways of connecting information and reality, perhaps the bridge would not be built by us. We might only point to the shores.

What I would like to do, if anything, is not declare that I have built that bridge. It is to argue that the gap is interesting enough to deserve a look. Perhaps from someone with far stronger formal tools than mine. Perhaps from a machine that one day sees as compatible things that today seem only vaguely similar to us. Or perhaps from nobody, because there is nothing to find.

But that uncertainty is part of the point too.

<a id="vi-the-big-question"></a>

## VI. The Big Question

Here we are. The thread that started with Lewis and religion ends somewhere entirely different, although “ends” may be the wrong word, since nothing here is really resolved.

Beneath every section, the anthropocentric trap, the code beneath the skin, the next rung, the limits of entropy, the cracks in the bridge, there is a question that will not go away:

Lewis asked: *without God, what anchors morality?*
Freud asked: *with God, what anchors reality?*
Asimov asked: *can entropy be reversed?*

I added my version somewhere in the middle: *what if the boundary between simulating and feeling were not a boundary at all?* But perhaps that too is only the same question dressed in the clothes available in 2026.

Beneath all these questions remains the one that has no answer and perhaps, structurally, cannot have one:

**Why is there something rather than nothing?**

Not “how.” Physics can answer that, or will, or perhaps already has in ways I have not fully understood. *Why.* Why does information exist? Why does it organize itself? Why, at least in our one observed case, does it become aware of itself? Why does it build flatworms, brains, AI, and then ask what it is?

I do not think the biggest question is “what is God?” or “is the universe a simulation?” or “will AI become conscious?” I think those are downstream questions. In different forms, they still ask whether the universe is like us: whether it has an author, an intention, a mind, a code, a will, a consciousness.

The question I keep returning to is different: *what happens when we stop needing the answer to take a human form?*

Every framework in this essay, including those I have relied on, was built by humans, using human concepts, to be understandable to humans. That is not a flaw. It might simply be a ceiling. Perhaps AI, or something after AI, will help us break through it. Perhaps not. Perhaps the ceiling is the world itself.

And beneath even that: **Why.**

If one day we became pure information, the why of that return would remain. If the universe were cyclical, the why of the cycle would remain. If AI discovered a new physics, the why of physics would remain. If every answer turned out to be another mirror, the why of the mirror would remain.

Not as the hidden cause of phenomena. The *why* does not make the worm regenerate or the universe repeat. Biology must explain the first process; cosmology must establish whether the second really exists. The question reappears when an explanation of *how* reaches its boundary: why are there laws capable of producing regeneration, organized matter, and experience?

Every attempt to answer might be wrong. But every attempt moves the edge of what we are capable of asking.

Perhaps that is the only honest place to stop.
